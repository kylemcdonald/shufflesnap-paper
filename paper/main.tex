\title{\textbf{ShuffleSnap: Multiscale Window Descent\\for Large Point-to-Grid Assignment}}
\author{Kyle McDonald}
\date{September 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Assigning planar points one-to-one to a regular grid with minimum total squared displacement is useful for image atlases and embedding visualization, but dense linear assignment requires quadratic memory. We present ShuffleSnap, a multiscale local-search method that repeatedly solves exact assignments within windows of at most $6{\times}6$ cells. Applying these windows to interleaved, strided subgrids permits long-range exchanges; halving the stride then refines the assignment at progressively smaller scales. Each round preserves the bijection, decreases the objective whenever a move is accepted, and uses linear work and memory for fixed window size. A logarithmic sequence of scales is followed by local polishing. On five nonuniform distribution families at $40{,}000$ points, median final gaps against high-accuracy auction references range from $0.006\%$ to $0.030\%$; the uniform family has a $2.85\%$ median gap, corresponding to $1.41\%$ excess RMS displacement. Random initialization gives lower median final costs than a raster-sorted start on $\StatInitWins$ of $\StatInitInstances$ tested nonuniform instance pairings. A $40{,}000$-point meander instance converges in $\StatFortyKConvergeS$~s (median), versus $\StatFortyKOracleS$~s for the reference solver; $1.28$ million points reach within $0.1\%$ of their converged cost in $\StatMillionWithinTenthS$~s. A billion-point stress test uses $\StatGigaPeakRssGiB$~GiB of memory. Comparisons with BSP-OT expose a tradeoff: construction wins at very short budgets, while further descent yields lower final costs. Code and benchmark data are available in the accompanying repositories.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A recurring task in data visualization is to snap a 2D point cloud onto a regular grid: start from an embedding of a large collection---images, documents, audio clips---produced by Isomap, t-SNE, or UMAP \cite{tenenbaum2000,vandermaaten2008,mcinnes2018}, and give every item its own cell of an image atlas. The grid removes overdraw and overlap while the assignment preserves as much of the embedding's geometry as possible. Formally, with points $p_1, \ldots, p_n$ in the plane and grid cell centers $g_1, \ldots, g_m$, $m \ge n$, an \emph{assignment} gives every point its own cell, with no cell used twice. Writing $a(i)$ for the cell of point $i$, we want the assignment minimizing the total squared displacement
\begin{equation}
\cost(a) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} \bigl\| p_i - g_{a(i)} \bigr\|^2.
\label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
Our main setting has $m=n$. When $m>n$, we optimize over a predetermined subset of $n$ occupied cells (Section~\ref{sec:masks}), rather than choosing that subset jointly with the assignment. With the target set fixed, this is a linear assignment problem, solvable exactly by the Hungarian method, shortest augmenting paths, or auctions \cite{kuhn1955,jonker1987,bertsekas1988}. The trouble is scale. The dense formulation stores $nm$ costs---$32$~GiB of float64 at $n=65{,}536$, $7.3$~TiB at one million---and dense exact solvers run in $O(n^3)$ worst-case time. Computing costs on demand removes the memory wall but not the time wall. Meanwhile the atlases people actually want to build keep growing: hundreds of thousands to millions of items.

This paper shows that near-optimal solutions to \eqref{eq:objective} are much cheaper than they look, using small exact solves and a multiscale neighborhood schedule.

\textbf{Ingredient 1: window moves.} Take any legal assignment. Choose a small window of the grid---at most $6{\times}6$ cells---and solve the assignment problem \emph{restricted to that window} exactly: the points currently occupying the window's cells are optimally re-matched to those same cells. A $36$-cell exact LAP takes microseconds. The global cost can only decrease, the assignment stays legal, and windows that do not share cells can be solved in parallel. Sweeping shifted windows across the grid is a strong local optimizer.

\textbf{Ingredient 2: strides.} A local window can move a point only a bounded number of cells per phase. To permit longer moves, split the grid into interleaved subgrids by taking every $s$-th row and column, and apply the same windows within each subgrid. A $6{\times}6$ window at stride $s$ spans $5s{+}1$ cells per axis. Halving the stride from a coarse scale to one exposes progressively smaller neighborhoods, without constructing or storing a dense cost matrix. The schedule is inspired by shell sort's gap sequence \cite{shell1959}; it also resembles multigrid smoothing \cite{brandt1977}, although here the neighborhoods change while the assignment remains at full resolution. These analogies motivate the schedule, not a convergence-rate guarantee.

Together these form \emph{multiscale window descent} (Section~\ref{sec:method}): seed with anything legal, run one round per stride from coarse to fine, then repeat stride-$1$ rounds until nothing changes. Our experiments (Section~\ref{sec:eval}) show:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A random start is effective.} On $200{\times}200$ meander instances, a random permutation reaches $0.1\%$ above the auction reference in a median of $\StatConvRoundsToTenth$ rounds ($\StatConvRandomTimeToTenthS$~s). Random starts yield lower median final costs than raster-sorted starts on $\StatInitWins$ of $\StatInitInstances$ tested nonuniform family--size--instance pairings (Section~\ref{sec:ablations}).
  \item \textbf{Small empirical gaps.} At $40$k points, five nonuniform families have median final gaps of $0.006$--$0.030\%$. Uniform inputs have larger relative gaps because their reference costs are much smaller; we also report the corresponding RMS interpretation (Section~\ref{sec:distributions}).
  \item \textbf{Large-scale feasibility.} Rounds have linear cost for fixed window size. The largest test uses a $\StatGigaSide{\times}\StatGigaSide$ grid with $\StatGigaNBillion$~billion points and $\StatGigaPeakRssGiB$~GiB of peak process memory. It reaches within $0.1\%$ of its budget-limited final cost in $\StatGigaWithinTenthMin$~minutes.
\end{itemize}

Our contributions are (1) multiscale window descent using exact small LAPs on strided tilings, with feasibility preservation, monotone accepted moves, parallel phases, and linear memory on fully occupied grids; (2) an evaluation of random and structured initializations, distribution sensitivity, schedules, window sizes, and thread counts; and (3) an open-source implementation, \shufflesnap{}, with benchmark data and figure-generation scripts.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Exact and approximate geometric assignment.} The LAP can be solved using the Hungarian method \cite{kuhn1955}, Jonker--Volgenant shortest augmenting paths \cite{jonker1987}, the rectangular variant \cite{crouse2016} used by SciPy \cite{virtanen2020}, or auctions \cite{bertsekas1988}. Geometric algorithms exploit structure beyond a dense cost matrix: Vaidya \cite{vaidya1989}, Raghvendra--Agarwal \cite{raghvendra2020}, and Gattani et al.\ \cite{gattani2023} study Euclidean matching. Their guarantees for the sum of Euclidean distances do not directly establish the same guarantees for our squared-distance objective. For planar RMS matching, which minimizes the sum of squared distances, Lahn--Raghvendra \cite{lahn2020} give an $\varepsilon$-approximation in $O(n^{5/4}\operatorname{poly}(\log n,1/\varepsilon))$ time. Our method trades such formal guarantees for a small parallel kernel and empirical speed. We use a floating-point auction implementation as a high-accuracy reference, with the validation and limitations described in Section~\ref{sec:protocol}.

\textbf{Optimal transport.} Entropic regularization \cite{cuturi2013,flamary2021} scales OT to large problems but produces soft couplings, not one-to-one assignments; extracting a bijection requires an additional rounding or assignment step. Multiscale OT solvers \cite{merigot2011,schmitzer2016} solve coarsened problems and refine, using hierarchical representations or sparse candidate sets, with different algorithmic machinery. Most recently, BSP-OT \cite{genest2025} constructs sparse transport plans and bijections in loglinear time by recursively splitting both measures with matched counts and merging randomized constructions; it is a scalable constructor that targets the same squared-distance objective, and we compare against it directly in Section~\ref{sec:bspot}. Our method never coarsens the problem or the measure; it always works on the full-resolution assignment and instead coarsens the \emph{neighborhood structure} of a local search, which is what makes it a dozen lines of logic around one small exact solver.

\textbf{Grid layouts in visualization.} Snapping embeddings to grids is folklore in the visualization community. RasterFairy \cite{klingemann2016} is the canonical tool: it \emph{constructs} the assignment top-down by recursive balanced splitting and stops there---the result is never refined against \eqref{eq:objective}, and Section~\ref{sec:convergence} measures what that leaves on the table. IsoMatch \cite{fried2015} assigns via dense LAP at small scale. Self-organizing maps \cite{kohonen1990} and kernelized sorting \cite{quadrianto2009} learn grid arrangements from high-dimensional data directly. Closest in spirit, Linear Assignment Sorting (LAS/FLAS) \cite{barthel2023} improves visually sorted grids with local exact assignments over neighborhoods of decreasing radius, and has been parallelized to sort millions of Gaussian-splat parameters \cite{morgenstern2024}; cluster-aware grid layouts \cite{zhou2024} optimize perceptual criteria. LAS/FLAS optimize feature-space arrangements using evolving target maps, whereas cluster-aware layouts additionally address cluster compactness and shape. Their objectives differ from the fixed planar displacement considered here. Our setting is deliberately narrower---the input is already a 2D embedding, the objective is fixed---which is what makes exact per-window solves, global monotonicity, and comparisons against high-accuracy LAP reference costs possible. Balanced and constrained clustering \cite{malinen2014,bradley2000} underlie the exact-cardinality splits that construction methods require.

Recent scalable visualization systems also address different output requirements. Sca$^2$Gri \cite{frey2025} selects representative glyphs for a limited grid with displacement constraints; its linear scaling in input points assumes that the displayed grid remains much smaller than the input. Here every point must retain its own cell, so both the number of targets and the output size grow with $n$.


\section{Multiscale Window Descent}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Setting}
In the benchmarks, each coordinate axis is independently rescaled to the grid's coordinate range; this preprocessing can change aspect ratio and therefore defines the geometry being optimized. The API accepts coordinates without applying this normalization. Grid cells are laid out on a regular lattice, inset from the square's edge by a small margin ($0.03$ in all experiments). The assignment is stored both ways: $a(i)$ gives point $i$'s cell, and an owner array gives each cell's point ($-1$ for empty). The objective is \eqref{eq:objective}. Squared distance penalizes large displacements: it charges a few long teleports far more than many one-cell nudges, and long teleports are what visibly tear a layout. For reference cost $C_{\mathrm{ref}}$, we report the \emph{relative gap} $100\,(\cost(a)/C_{\mathrm{ref}}-1)$, in percent. At reference sizes $C_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the validated auction cost (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}); large-scale comparisons explicitly use a run's final cost instead.

\subsection{Window moves}
A \emph{window} is a small block of grid cells, at most $6\times6$. The \emph{window move} collects the points currently assigned to a window's cells and re-matches them optimally to those same cells with a dense Jonker--Volgenant solve of size at most $36$ (Figure~\ref{fig:windows}, left). The current matching is one candidate the solver can return, and we apply the result only when it is \emph{strictly} cheaper, so every applied move lowers the global cost; because a move only shuffles points within its own window, the assignment stays legal. Strict decrease over a finite set of assignments guarantees termination in exact arithmetic. In the implementation a move must reduce its computed window cost by more than $10^{-12}$ times the incumbent window cost; this rejects numerical ties. Reported fixed points are relative to this acceptance tolerance and the scheduled windows.

Windows that share no cells touch disjoint points, so a whole \emph{tiling} of side-by-side windows can be solved concurrently and applied without conflict (Figure~\ref{fig:windows}, center). We call one such tiling a \emph{phase}. Together with the unshifted tiling, shifts by half a window horizontally, vertically, and in both directions give four phases that overlap one another; Figure~\ref{fig:windows}, right shows the phase shifted in both directions. Its complete windows cross the previous boundaries, so improvements propagate between neighboring windows. At the far grid edges, a shifted tiling is clipped into smaller disjoint windows (for example, $6{\times}3$, $3{\times}6$, and $3{\times}3$); cells in the omitted leading half-window still participate in the unshifted phases. Four phases make one \emph{round}. One round solves ${\sim}\,4n/36$ windows and costs $O(n\,w^4)$ arithmetic at window size $w$ ($O(w^6)$ per window over $n/w^2$ windows, four times), with independent window solves inside each phase.

Center: all nine disjoint windows in one unshifted phase, solved in parallel. Right: the actual half-window-offset phase on this $18{\times}18$ grid---four $6{\times}6$ windows, two $6{\times}3$, two $3{\times}6$, and one $3{\times}3$. The blank leading half-window remains covered by the unshifted phases.}
  \label{fig:windows}
\end{figure}

\subsection{The locality bottleneck}
A point can move at most $w-1$ cells per axis in one adjacent-cell window phase. With four phases per round, traveling $k$ cells requires at least $\lceil k/[4(w-1)]\rceil$ rounds, if the descent can reach that destination at all. This is a lower bound on transport through local moves, not an upper bound on convergence: a local fixed point may prevent the necessary exchanges altogether. On the $200{\times}200$ meander experiments, local-only refinement from the raster start stops at a median gap of $\StatConvLocalFinal\%$ (Figure~\ref{fig:convergence}). Strided windows address this limited reach by permitting exchanges between distant cells.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{strided_windows.png}
  \caption{A $6{\times}6$ window at stride $4$ (left) covers every fourth cell and spans a $21{\times}21$ block of the grid; at stride $1$ (right) the same window covers adjacent cells. The stride-$s$ cells split into $s^2$ interleaved subgrids (a second one shown in gray), each tiled by disjoint windows, so each round has linear work for fixed window size, regardless of stride.}
  \label{fig:strided}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Strided windows}
The repair is to let windows act at every length scale. Fix a stride $s \ge 1$ and stripe the grid: take every $s$-th row and every $s$-th column, starting from some offset. The cells where they intersect form a regular subgrid, and the $s^2$ possible offsets give $s^2$ interleaved subgrids that together cover every cell exactly once. Now tile each subgrid with the same $6{\times}6$ windows, shifted through the same four phases (Figure~\ref{fig:strided}). A window at stride $s$ still contains at most $36$ cells, but they span $5s{+}1$ rows and columns of the full grid: one exact solve can move a point $5s$ cells at once. Crucially, the accounting does not change---each cell belongs to at most one window per phase, and the unshifted phase covers the grid. Boundary clipping changes the number and sizes of windows, but a round still has $O(n w^4)$ work on a fully occupied grid and retains the same parallelism and descent property. A stride-$s$ round \emph{is} a stride-$1$ round applied simultaneously to $s^2$ interleaved subproblems.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Multiscale window descent}
\label{alg:descent}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $a \gets$ any legal assignment \Comment{default: a seeded random permutation (Section~\ref{sec:ablations})}
\State $S \gets (s_{\max}, s_{\max}/2, \ldots, 2, 1)$ \Comment{$s_{\max}$: smallest power of 2 with $6\,s_{\max} \ge$ grid extent}
\For{$s \in S$, then $s = 1$ repeatedly}
  \For{phase offset $\phi \in \{(0,0), (0,3), (3,0), (3,3)\}$}
    \State solve all windows of the stride-$s$, offset-$\phi$ tiling exactly, in parallel
  \EndFor
  \If{$s = 1$ and no assignment changed} \Return $a$ \Comment{converged}
  \EndIf
  \If{optional time budget exhausted} \Return $a$
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The schedule (Algorithm~\ref{alg:descent}) chooses the smallest power-of-two stride satisfying $w s\geq L$ for each axis of length $L$, then halves each stride until one. This criterion sets a coarse scale; it need not produce a complete window spanning the entire axis. On rectangular grids the strides are independent: a $50{\times}1000$ grid starts at $(16,256)$. After the descent, stride-$1$ rounds repeat until a full round accepts no move. We call this a \emph{scheduled-window fixed point}: none of the four scheduled tilings improves the assignment beyond the numerical acceptance tolerance. The initial descent contains $O(\log L)$ rounds and costs $O(n w^4\log L)$, with $L$ the larger grid dimension; $T$ additional polishing rounds cost $O(T n w^4)$. We do not bound $T$ or the final optimality gap. Shell sort and multigrid provide useful analogies for long-range corrections, but their analyses do not transfer to this nonconvex permutation search.

Figure~\ref{fig:action} shows the descent working on a $512{\times}512$ grid: each coarse round fixes structure at its own scale, and the final polish is imperceptible at figure resolution.

From the default random seed---pure noise in this rendering---the stride-$128$ round fixes global placement, intermediate strides fix intermediate scales, and stride-$1$ polishing is invisible at this resolution.}

\subsection{Predetermined occupied cells}
\label{sec:masks}
When $m>n$, the occupied cell subset is fixed before optimization. Every window considers only cells occupied in the initial assignment and re-matches their points to those same cells, so its LAP remains square and no point can enter an unused cell. The high-level API chooses a deterministic subset once when a grid has spare cells; a boolean mask can instead prescribe the usable region explicitly, including circular or cut-corner atlases (Figure~\ref{fig:masks}).

\end{figure}

\subsection{Implementation}
\label{sec:impl}
\shufflesnap{}'s kernel is implemented in C++ behind a small Python API. Per phase, windows are enumerated on the fly from two per-axis band tables ($O(n)$ index arithmetic, trivial next to the solves) and chunked across hardware threads; each worker applies its own results in place, which is safe because a phase's windows are disjoint. The window solver is a fixed-size square JV specialized to $\le 36$ cells with stack-allocated state. Costs are computed from coordinates on demand; storage includes the points, assignment and owner arrays, and grid axes. It is $O(n+m)$ for $n$ points and $m$ available grid cells, hence linear in $n$ when $m=O(n)$; phase enumeration also depends on $m$ for sparse masks. Thread scheduling never affects results (phases apply disjoint window results), so run-to-convergence output is deterministic given inputs and parameters.

The default window size and schedule are fixed: by default the solver runs until a full stride-$1$ round finds nothing to improve, yielding a fixed point under the implemented tolerance. The ordinary convergence experiments reached this condition; the billion-point stress run was stopped by its budget. An optional wall-clock budget stops early for interactive use; because the process is front-loaded (Section~\ref{sec:scaling}), early stops cost little quality. The default four tilings are deliberately sparse; an optional \texttt{polish\_all\_offsets} sweep tests every complete $6{\times}6$ window placement once at additional cost. The high-level \texttt{snap\_to\_grid(points)} picks a near-square grid when none is given (or takes a mask), seeds it with a deterministic random assignment (supported by the tested ablations---Section~\ref{sec:ablations}), runs Algorithm~\ref{alg:descent} to convergence, optionally performs that dense local polish, and returns grid positions, the assignment, and the grid shape.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
Experiments run on a desktop with a \StatMachine{} ($\StatThreads$ hardware threads, $\StatMemory$~GB RAM), Linux, and a C++ compiler using \texttt{-O3}; descent uses all threads unless stated. Synthetic instances come from six families: normalized Gaussian random walks (\emph{meander}, the default), uniform samples, two blobs, a ring, a spiral, and a mixture of five unequal Gaussians. Unless explicitly ablated, initialization is a deterministic random permutation. Ordinary synthetic comparisons use instance seeds $\{0,1,2\}$ and report medians, with min--max bands where visible. Large stress runs and the MNIST example use a single instance; initialization uses five random permutations per instance. Timings are machine- and implementation-specific. The convergence/scaling traces include initialization where stated; distribution and ablation tables report the solver's measured cleanup time. The BSP-OT time-matched experiment excludes input construction and ShuffleSnap initialization and retains only completed round checkpoints, as detailed in Section~\ref{sec:bspot}.

Reference costs are computed with an on-demand, epsilon-scaled floating-point auction solver. Its final epsilon is $\max(10^{-12}/n,\operatorname{ulp}(C_{\mathrm{scale}}))$, where $C_{\mathrm{scale}}=\max(1,\max_{ij}|c_{ij}|)$. In exact arithmetic epsilon-complementary slackness would bound the additive error by $n\varepsilon$; the stored runs do not include an independently verified dual certificate, so we do not claim certified optimality. On $12$ meander instances of $400$--$4{,}096$ points where dense JV, SciPy, and auction all finish, their relative cost differences are below $\StatOracleMaxRelErr$. We therefore use the auction values as high-accuracy numerical references. Cached references cover all six families at $96{\times}96$ and $200{\times}200$, and smaller meander instances used in scaling. Above $40$k points we report progress relative to each run's own final cost, distinguishing converged from budget-limited runs.


Code, stored benchmark results, and figure-generation scripts are available at \url{https://github.com/kylemcdonald/shufflesnap-paper}; the native solver is at \url{https://github.com/kylemcdonald/shufflesnap}. Headline statistics and the comparison table are generated from the stored results. The accompanying solver source is ShuffleSnap~0.3.0, commit \href{https://github.com/kylemcdonald/shufflesnap/tree/59968704450dfcb6206cb3c835193e60c3605eff}{\texttt{5996870}}. The $18$ distribution outcomes were reproduced with this source; most timing measurements predate the rename. The convergence experiment was rerun with the package-default permutation for consistency. The paper repository records that rerun separately and documents the environment, build commands, and remaining historical provenance limitations.

\subsection{Convergence, and the role of initialization}
\label{sec:convergence}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{convergence.png}
  \caption{Gap to the auction reference on $200{\times}200$ meander instances (median of $3$; right panel adds initialization time and a min--max band). Multiscale descent converges to approximately $0.003$--$0.021\%$ in a fraction of a second from either start; the same windows without strides (``local only'') terminate near $0.5$--$0.6\%$ for the two local-only starts, even from RasterFairy's construction.}
  \label{fig:convergence}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:convergence} is the paper's central experiment, and it separates the two ingredients of Section~\ref{sec:method}. From a random permutation---median starting gap around $500\%$---multiscale descent crosses $1\%$ in five rounds, $0.1\%$ in $\StatConvRoundsToTenth$, and converges at $\StatConvRandomFinal\%$ within a quarter of a second. The raster start follows the same trajectory---one coarse round erases most of the difference between a random and a sorted start---but has a higher median final cost in this experiment; across the nonuniform initialization ablation, the raster-to-random median gap ratio is $\StatInitMedianRatio$ (the initialization ablation of Section~\ref{sec:ablations} quantifies both). These tests support using a random shuffle as the default initialization.

Removing the strides---the same solver, windows of adjacent cells only---tells the opposite story and illustrates the locality limitation of Section~\ref{sec:method}: from the raster start, local-only refinement needs ${\sim}660$ rounds and $14$~s just to cross $1\%$, and reaches a scheduled-window fixed point after ${\sim}1{,}000$ rounds at $\StatConvLocalFinal\%$. Nor can a better start rescue it. RasterFairy \cite{klingemann2016} itself\footnote{We run the released \texttt{rasterfairy} package. Its splits sort integer-cast coordinates---it expects pixel-scale input such as t-SNE screen positions---so point clouds are scaled up before the call; its output is unaffected.} is a far stronger construction---it starts at $\StatConvRasterFairySeedGap\%$ where the raster sort starts at ${\sim}210\%$---yet local windows from that start terminate at $\StatConvRasterFairyLocalFinal\%$, indistinguishable from the raster start, because the misplacements that remain are precisely the long-range ones local windows cannot execute. On these meander instances, better initial cost does not translate into a better final cost under the tested local schedule. (Section~\ref{sec:bspot} reports a related observation: on three meander instances, BSP-OT initialization converges to a higher final cost than random initialization.)

\subsection{Scaling}
\label{sec:scaling}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{scaling.png}
  \caption{Left: wall time versus problem size on meander instances from the default random permutation (median of $3$ up to $640$k points; single stress runs above). Where auction references exist ($n \le 40$k), time to $0.1\%$ reference gap; everywhere, time to full convergence and time to reach within $0.1\%$ of the converged cost. BSP-OT's construction time is shown for context; quality at reference sizes is evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:bspot}. Right: per-round time is linear in $n$.}
  \label{fig:scaling}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:scaling} sweeps meander instances from $5$k to $4.2$M points. Three regimes are visible. Against the auction reference solver, the crossover is immediate: at $5$k points the oracle takes ${\sim}5$~s while descent converges in well under a second; at $40$k the oracle needs $\StatFortyKOracleS$~s versus $\StatFortyKConvergeS$~s to \emph{converge} (final gap $\StatFortyKGap\%$) and $\StatFortyKToTenthS$~s to $0.1\%$. Second, per-round cost is linear in $n$ across three orders of magnitude (right panel)---there is no $n^2$ anywhere in the pipeline---and memory follows suit: the largest sweep run ($\StatLargestN$ points) peaks at $\StatLargestPeakRssGiB$~GiB of process memory, benchmark harness included, where its dense cost matrix alone would occupy $\StatLargestDenseGiB$~TiB. Third, every run reaches a scheduled-window fixed point, before its configured time budget: $\StatSixHundredKConvergeS$~s at $640$k points, $\StatMillionConvergeS$~s at $1.3$M, $\StatLargestConvergeMin$ minutes at $4.2$M. The anytime profile is heavily front-loaded, so an early stop costs little: the $1.3$M-point assignment is within $0.1\%$ of its converged cost after $\StatMillionWithinTenthS$~s, and the $4.2$M-point assignment after $\StatLargestWithinTenthS$~s---the long tail to full convergence buys only the final tenth of a percent (cf.\ Figure~\ref{fig:action}).

The largest problem we have attempted is a single billion-point instance: a $\StatGigaSide{\times}\StatGigaSide$ grid holding $\StatGigaNBillion$~billion meander points (seed $0$), on the same desktop. At this scale we cap the run at $\StatGigaBudgetH$~hours rather than wait for a zero-change round, so quality is measured against the run's own final cost. The behavior extrapolates cleanly from Figure~\ref{fig:scaling}: the random-permutation seed takes $\StatGigaSeedS$~s, rounds stay linear in $n$ ($\StatGigaPerRoundMin$~minutes each), and the anytime profile keeps its front-loaded shape---the assignment is within $1\%$ of its final cost after $\StatGigaWithinOneMin$~minutes, $0.1\%$ after $\StatGigaWithinTenthMin$~minutes, $0.01\%$ after $\StatGigaWithinHundredthMin$~minutes, and $0.001\%$ after $\StatGigaWithinThousandthH$~hours, with the rest of the $\StatGigaRounds$-round run polishing below that. Peak process memory is $\StatGigaPeakRssGiB$~GiB, including coordinates, index arrays, and benchmark overhead, where the dense cost matrix would occupy $\StatGigaDenseMTiB$~million~TiB.

\subsection{Robustness across distributions}
\label{sec:distributions}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{distributions.png}
  \caption{The six instance families at $40$k points ($200{\times}200$, seed $0$), solved four ways. Each cell takes its occupant's color, multiplied by a graph-paper grid in input space (spacing $0.1$, thickness $0.02$, half brightness), so the wavy lines trace each solution's distortion; the annotation under each grid is that solution's gap above the auction reference and its solve time. The line patterns expose differences in displacement and spatial coherence. At this rendering resolution, descent closely resembles the auction reference; this visual comparison is qualitative. Uniform has a small reference cost and a larger relative gap.}
  \label{fig:distributions}
\end{figure}

The five nonuniform families show consistently small median gaps (Figure~\ref{fig:distributions}). At $40$k points, nonuniform family medians land between $0.006\%$ and $0.03\%$, and every run converges in at most $\StatDistMaxTime$~s. Rings and spirals, which defeat axis-aligned recursive splitting, sit at $0.01$--$0.03\%$; two separated blobs, the classic failure case for local search (points trapped on the wrong side of the gap), converge to $0.02\%$ because coarse strides move points between blobs as easily as within them; starting gaps up to $\StatDistMaxSeedGap{\times}$ the optimum are erased along the way. The worst nonuniform run (one two-blobs instance) converges at $0.13\%$. Uniform's median gap is $\approx 2.85\%$, with an individual maximum of $4.71\%$: a uniform cloud nearly \emph{is} a grid, so the reference cost is small relative to the other families, making the ratio sensitive to small absolute errors. A $2.85\%$ cost gap corresponds to $100(\sqrt{1.0285}-1)\approx1.41\%$ excess RMS displacement, rising to $2.33\%$ for the worst uniform run.

\subsection{Comparison with BSP-OT}
\label{sec:bspot}

\begin{table}[tbp]
  \centering
  \caption{BSP-OT (the tested setting of $16$ merged plans) versus ShuffleSnap from its default random permutation on the six $40$k-point ($200{\times}200$) families, on the same machine. Both optimize the same objective; gaps are against the same auction references. The time-matched ShuffleSnap column comes from fresh runs: for each instance it retains the last fully completed checkpoint at or before that instance's measured BSP-OT solve time $t_{\mathrm{BSP}}$ (medians of $3$ instances and $5$ timing repeats). The final two ShuffleSnap columns are independent runs to scheduled-window convergence. Uniform's gaps are inflated by its near-zero optimal cost for both methods (Section~\ref{sec:distributions}).}
  \label{tab:bspot}
  \input{bspot_table.tex}
\end{table}

Our principal scalable comparison is BSP-OT \cite{genest2025}, which builds a bijection between two point sets in loglinear time: recursively split both sets into halves of matched size along data-adapted directions until singletons pair up, then merge several randomized constructions, keeping cost-decreasing swaps. It seeks to reduce the same squared-distance objective, and its official implementation is parallel C++,\footnote{\url{https://github.com/baptiste-genest/BSP-OT}, evaluated at the Gaussian bijective-matching mode with $16$ merged plans via the header-only release; our driver is \texttt{solvers/cpp/bspot\_grid.cpp}.} so it makes a useful baseline. Both implementations use multiple threads on the same $24$-thread machine: BSP-OT parallelizes across its $16$ plans through OpenMP, while ShuffleSnap parallelizes across windows. The original table runs do not record the OpenMP thread limit separately. Table~\ref{tab:bspot} compares the two on the six families at $40$k points.

The two methods sit at different points on the quality--time curve. BSP-OT delivers its answer in $0.04$--$0.07$~s---several times faster than full convergence of descent---at gaps of $1.5$--$2.3\%$ on typical families, $100$--$400\times$ the converged descent gaps. Its spread across geometry is also much wider: $0.07\%$ on two separated blobs, whose isotropic clusters are ideal for its splits, but $13\%$ on the spiral, whose thin curved structure crosses every splitting plane. The time-matched column makes the sub-$0.1$-second tradeoff explicit: at each individual BSP-OT runtime, ShuffleSnap has a lower median gap on \StatBspotTimeMatchedWins{} of \StatBspotTimeMatchedTotal{} families (on meander, \StatBspotTimeMatchedMeanderGap\% versus BSP-OT's \StatBspotMeanderGap\%). The result is sensitive to round granularity at these budgets: completing one additional round can substantially change the reported gap. BSP-OT has the lower family-median gap at the measured short budgets on all six families; further rounds are what carry ShuffleSnap to its much lower converged floor. Extra randomized plans saturate quickly ($64$ plans: $1.6\%$ on meander at ${\sim}2.5\times$ the time). In the scaling experiment, BSP-OT takes $\StatBspotLargestTime$~s for $4.2$ million points; descent reaches within $0.1\%$ of its own converged cost in $\StatLargestWithinTenthS$~s. These large runs lack optimal reference costs, so the latter threshold is not an optimality-gap comparison.

We also tested BSP-OT as an initializer on three $200{\times}200$ meander instances. The resulting median final gap is $\StatBspotSeedFinal\%$, versus $\StatConvRandomFinal\%$ for random initialization in the convergence experiment. This does not establish a general ordering of constructors and random starts: the tested sample is small and the result depends on the descent schedule. One possible explanation is that a structured seed places the search in a different basin before the coarse scales have been visited.

The comparison cuts both ways. BSP-OT handles arbitrary dimensions, partial matchings, and sparse couplings---none of which we attempt---and when a percent-level map in milliseconds is the goal, it is the right tool. ShuffleSnap specializes to grid targets, which permit inexpensive strided tilings; BSP-OT supports a broader class of targets and transport problems.

\subsection{Ablations}
\label{sec:ablations}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{ablations.png}
  \caption{Left: window size trades per-round cost against reach; $w{\le}3$ does not reach $0.1\%$ on all instances, and $w \in \{4,5,6\}$ are all reasonable. Right: schedule variants run to convergence ($200{\times}200$, median of $3$). The default and cycled coarse-to-fine schedules give similar, low median gaps; the ablation is empirical, not a necessity result.}
  \label{fig:ablations}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Window size} (Figure~\ref{fig:ablations}, left). Per-window work grows as $O(w^6)$, but larger windows buy proportionally more reach and better local optima: $w{=}2$ converges near $0.3\%$ and $w{=}3$ near $0.1\%$, while $w \in \{4,5,6\}$ all reach $0.1\%$ in $0.07$--$0.11$~s at this scale (differences within noise) and the converged gap keeps improving with $w$ ($0.02\%$ at $w{=}4$, $0.006\%$ at $w{=}6$). We ship $w=6$, the largest our fixed-size kernel supports.

\textbf{Schedule} (Figure~\ref{fig:ablations}, right). Cycling the full descent four times reduces the gap by less than $0.00002$ percentage points on each of the three meander instances, while adding rounds. One descent captures almost all of this improvement in these tests. The ratio-$4$ variant skips intermediate scales and finishes around $0.08\%$; half-depth, fine-to-coarse, and local-only schedules finish near $0.5\%$. These observations motivate the chosen schedule, but neither prove that a single descent is sufficient on all inputs nor show that a stride-$1$ fixed point is stable under every possible coarse move.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{init.png}
  \caption{Initialization ablation. Left: converged gap from the raster-sorted seed versus a random permutation on the six families ($200{\times}200$; $3$ instances each, $5$ permutations per instance). Right: rounds to strict convergence on meander instances as the grid grows (median of $3$); the sorted seed's convergence tail stretches with scale while the random seed's does not.}
  \label{fig:init}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Initialization} (Figure~\ref{fig:init}). Random initialization is inexpensive; it also gives lower final costs than the raster start in this ablation. Pairing each raster-sorted run against five random permutations of the same instance, the random seed converges strictly lower on $\StatInitWins$ of $\StatInitInstances$ family--size--instance pairings (the five nonuniform families at $96{\times}96$ and $200{\times}200$), with a median $\StatInitMedianRatio\times$ lower converged gap ($\StatInitRatioTwoHundred\times$ at $200{\times}200$); on the degenerate uniform family the two are within noise of each other. The advantage grows with scale and shows up in time as well as quality: at $\StatInitLargestSide^2$ ($640$k points) the raster seed needs $\StatInitRoundsLargestRaster$ rounds ($\StatInitTimeLargestRasterS$~s) to reach a scheduled-window fixed point where the random seed needs $\StatInitRoundsLargestRandom$ ($\StatInitTimeLargestRandomS$~s), and the random seed still reaches a lower final cost on $\StatInitScaleWins$ of $\StatInitScaleTotal$ instance--size pairs (paired comparison of converged costs; no exact references above $40$k). These paired results motivate the deterministic random default; they do not imply that random initialization is best for every instance or schedule.

\textbf{Threads.} Measured round throughput is $\StatThreadSpeedup{\times}$ higher on $24$ threads than on one ($512{\times}512$; budgeted runs, so the thread counts complete different mixes of coarse and fine rounds). Thread count does not change run-to-convergence results.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{No approximation or iteration bound.} A scheduled-window fixed point need not be globally optimal. We provide no worst-case approximation ratio or bound on the number of polishing rounds. The distribution-dependent gaps in Section~\ref{sec:distributions} are empirical, with larger relative errors on uniform inputs. Our floating-point reference costs are cross-validated numerically rather than accompanied by independently checked dual certificates.

\textbf{Relative gaps degenerate when the optimum is tiny.} The uniform family shows this; relative gaps should be accompanied by absolute or RMS displacement measures.

\textbf{Scope.} The method optimizes \eqref{eq:objective} and nothing else: it inherits whatever the embedding got wrong, and it does not optimize high-dimensional neighborhood metrics directly, unlike LAS/FLAS-style systems \cite{barthel2023}. It is specialized to regular grids; the strided-subgrid construction extends naturally to any grid-structured target set, but irregular targets would need a different way of generating windows. With spare grid cells, the occupied subset is fixed in advance: the method does not solve the unrestricted rectangular assignment problem. A single real embedding and three synthetic instances per family provide limited evidence about visual quality or broad generalization.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

ShuffleSnap combines small exact assignment solves with a coarse-to-fine schedule of strided grid neighborhoods. Accepted moves preserve feasibility and reduce the objective; fixed-size windows keep each round linear in work and memory on fully occupied grids. The experiments show that a random permutation is an effective starting point and that further descent can reach substantially lower costs than fast construction methods, at a higher runtime. The initial schedule has logarithmically many scales, but convergence time and final accuracy remain empirical properties. The implementation makes million-point bijections practical and supports a billion-point stress test, providing a simple option when exact certificates are unnecessary.

Figure~\ref{fig:billion-interp} renders the $\StatGigaNBillion$-billion-point
random-init solve of Section~\ref{sec:scaling} in a way that exposes exactly where the
transport map is discontinuous. Every point is drawn most of the way toward
its assigned cell: wherever neighboring cells hold points from nearby source
positions, the partially collapsed cloud stays dense and the image stays
colored; where neighboring assignments separate in this visualization, white seams open up. The seams are sparse,
smooth, and dendritic, which provides a qualitative view of spatial coherence, rather than a quantitative neighborhood-preservation test at this scale.

For context on the same instance, we also ran BSP-OT (Section~\ref{sec:bspot})
at this scale. The evaluated official implementation does not fit in our memory budget at its tested $16$-plan setting: each stored matching keeps
a plan and its inverse, projection records are $16$-byte pairs, and merging
materializes per-point cost vectors, which puts a single plan near
$\StatGigaBspotStockOnePlanGiB$~GiB and the $16$-plan setting beyond
$\StatGigaBspotStockDefaultGiB$~GiB at $n = 10^9$ (both extrapolated from
footprints we measured at $10^8$), against $\StatMemory$~GB on our machine. We
therefore ported its single-plan Gaussian construction to a memory-lean driver
(\texttt{solvers/cpp/bspot\_grid\_giga.cpp}: packed $8$-byte projection
records, streamed slice moments, no inverse plan). On the stored $10^8$-point comparison, the port's single-plan cost is about $3\%$ above the official binary's. We then ran the port at a billion points. The single
plan builds its billion-point bijection in $\StatGigaBspotTimeMin$~minutes on
one thread (its parallelism is across plans) with a peak of
$\StatGigaBspotPeakRssGiB$~GiB, and lands $\StatGigaBspotExcessPct\%$ above
descent's final cost. Single plans are BSP-OT's weakest configuration; at
$10^8$ points, where the official $16$-plan configuration still fits
($\StatHundredMBspotDefaultGiB$~GiB with four OpenMP threads), $16$ merged plans land
$\StatHundredMBspotSixteenExcessPct\%$ above a descent run from the default
random permutation with a $500$~s requested budget on the same instance (the final completed round ends at $520$~s), and single plans
$\StatHundredMBspotOneExcessLoPct$--$\StatHundredMBspotOneExcessHiPct\%$.
The $10^8$ comparison retains summary measurements but not raw upstream logs or a pinned upstream revision; it is supporting evidence, not a reproducible equivalence test. Descent uses $24$ threads in these stress tests.
Descent's billion-point run (Section~\ref{sec:scaling}; $24$ threads,
$\StatGigaPeakRssGiB$~GiB peak) drops below BSP-OT's final cost by the end of
its round~$\StatGigaCrossBspotRound$, $\StatGigaCrossBspotMin$~minutes in, and
continues to within $0.1\%$ of its final cost at
$\StatGigaWithinTenthMin$~minutes. The pattern of Table~\ref{tab:bspot}
carries unchanged to $10^9$: the construction is fast and memory-comparable,
but additional descent lowers the cost. This stress-test comparison uses a custom single-plan port and different thread counts; it is not a time-matched comparison with the official $16$-plan implementation, and neither billion-point result has an optimality certificate.
