\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A common way to show a large image or document collection is to embed the items in
the plane (for example with t-SNE~\citep{vandermaaten2008tsne} or
UMAP~\citep{mcinnes2018umap}) and then snap every item to its own cell of a regular grid,
so that thumbnails tile the screen without overlap. Snapping is an assignment problem:
each point must receive a distinct cell, and we would like points to move as little as
possible. Measured as total squared displacement, the best snapping is a linear
assignment problem (LAP), and also a discrete optimal-transport problem with quadratic
cost~\citep{peyre2019computational}.

For a few thousand points, practitioners solve this LAP exactly with a dense
Jonker--Volgenant or Hungarian solver~\citep{markovtsev2017jvtsne,mcdonald2016cloudtogrid,
markovtsev_lapjv,kazmar_lap}. The dense cost matrix needs $8N^2$ bytes, which is
80\,GB at $N=100{,}000$, so these solvers stop being usable somewhere between
10{,}000 and 30{,}000 points. Beyond that, people turn to recursive-splitting heuristics
such as RasterFairy~\citep{klingemann2015rasterfairy} or to fast approximate transport
such as BSP-OT~\citep{genest2025bspot}.

This paper develops and evaluates \ShuffleSnap, a simple heuristic for this regime,
taking its name from Shellsort~\citep{shell1959highspeed}. Shellsort sorts interleaved
subsequences at a decreasing sequence of gaps. \ShuffleSnap\ does the same in 2D: at stride $s$
it splits the grid into $s^2$ interleaved subgrids and solves small \emph{exact}
assignment problems inside $8\times8$ windows of each subgrid, dividing $s$ by $\sqrt2$
until it reaches 1 (Figure~\ref{fig:method}). All work is at full resolution; nothing is
aggregated or downsampled.

We treat the idea as a hypothesis to test, not a result to promote. Our aim throughout
was to find where it fails. The main contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A careful implementation} with documented rules for boundaries,
  rectangular grids, surplus cells and masks. It is a parallel C++ kernel with a Python
  API whose results do not depend on the number of threads, and three presets: \emph{fast}
  ($5\times5$ windows), \emph{balanced} ($8\times8$, the default) and \emph{quality}
  ($12\times12$).
\item \textbf{A geometric window solver.} Each window is solved exactly, starting from dual
  potentials derived in closed form from per-axis optimal transport between the window's
  points and cells. This removes the conflicts that make clustered windows slow for
  standard solvers. It is 5.0--5.4$\times$ faster than the Hungarian method with a greedy start
  on clustered data and 2--2.4$\times$ faster on uniform points, with the same results. The
  saved time buys larger windows at the same running time.
\item \textbf{A certified exact solver that reaches $10^6$ points.} It solves min-cost
  flow on sparse candidate edges, then prices every edge of the \emph{dense} problem
  through a Lagrangian bound, adding edges until the bound meets the primal cost. It gives
  true optimality gaps, not progress relative to our own best answer, for all 202 benchmark
  instances (up to 200{,}000 points) and for uniform instances up to $10^6$ points. Where
  SciPy's dense solver fits, both found the same optimum on every instance: 600 small ones
  and all ten distributions at $10^4$ points. It turned out to be a strong practical
  baseline as well.
\item \textbf{Measured design choices.} The presets were chosen on a development set and
  every claim is evaluated on held-out instances. Window size is the main quality knob,
  and dividing the stride by $\sqrt2$ is best or close to best at every window size.
  Several intuitive ideas did not help:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item bisection starts make results \emph{worse}, and so do row-sorted starts on most inputs;
  \item polishing at coarser strides changes almost nothing, and merging independent runs
    helps less than a larger window;
  \item a Jonker--Volgenant window solver is slower, and an auction warm start helps less
    than the geometric one.
  \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{A diagnosis of the remaining error.} The gap to the optimum is concentrated
  in a few long closed loops of points, each displaced by about one cell. No window
  smaller than a loop can remove it. Masks with concave bays and grids with surplus
  cells expose the same mechanism at a larger scale.
\item \textbf{A benchmark against exact LAP, RasterFairy and BSP-OT.} We first verified
  each comparator's capabilities: we found and worked around coordinate truncation in the
  PyPI release of RasterFairy, and used its much stronger upstream version. The
  fast and default presets take about as long as BSP-OT with 16 and 64 plans, so the two
  methods can be compared at equal time: \ShuffleSnap\ is closer to the optimum on eight and
  nine of ten distributions. The benchmark covers varied and adversarial distributions,
  masks, prime point counts, two
  real UMAP embeddings with 70{,}000 points each, multiple seeds, matched-budget curves,
  and scaling to $10^6$ points, plus one run on $10^9$ points. We report cost, time, peak memory, and neighbourhood
  preservation, and we keep displacement and neighbourhood measures separate.
\end{itemize}

The implementation (\texttt{shufflesnap}) and this paper's experiment code, raw results
and figure scripts are released as two separate repositories.
