Table~\ref{tab:main} and Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff} summarize the held-out comparison at
$10^5$ points on one thread.

\paragraph{\ShuffleSnap.} The default (balanced) preset is within 0.06\% of the optimum on nine
of the ten distributions, and within 0.005\% on six of them. It needs a median of 2.3\,s on
one thread. The exception is uniform points (0.84\%), where the long loops of
Section~\ref{sec:anatomy} remain. The presets trade time for quality on every
distribution. Fast (median 0.77\,s) is within 0.16\% on the same nine distributions; quality
(median 6.7\,s) lowers the excess on uniform points to 0.44\%.

\paragraph{At equal time.} The fast preset takes about as long as BSP-OT with 16 plans (0.77\,s
against 0.71\,s), and the default a little less than BSP-OT with 64 plans (2.3\,s against
2.9\,s). At these matched times \ShuffleSnap\ is closer to the optimum on eight and on nine of
the ten distributions. The fast preset is at 2.6\% on uniform points, 0.013\% on the mixture
and 0.13\% on the checkerboard, where BSP-OT with 16 plans is at 45\%, 5.6\% and 50\%. The
default is at 0.84\%, 0.002\% and 0.057\% there, against 32\%, 3.7\% and 36\% for 64 plans.
BSP-OT is ahead only on two inputs whose structure suits its partitions: the near-line (0.008\%
against 0.010\% for fast; 0.0021\% against 0.0022\% for the default) and the 90/10 clusters
(0.032\% against 0.050\% for fast).

\paragraph{RasterFairy (upstream)} takes about 3\,s. Its recursive slicing, however, costs
17--425\% on seven of the ten distributions. It does well on two: the Gaussian (0.006\%) and
the 90/10 clusters (1.2\%).
The PyPI release, with pre-scaled inputs, gives similar or worse costs (e.g.\ 62\% vs.\ 45\%
on uniform points) and takes 80\,s at this size. Its targets at 50k and 100k points have a partially filled
last row, which sends it into its much slower mask code path; at $N=10^4$, a full
$100\times100$ square, it needs 0.03\,s.

\paragraph{RasterFairy's swap optimizer} needs a dense $N\times N$ distance matrix, so we ran
it only at $N=2{,}500$ (49\,MB), on the same ten distributions (Table~\ref{tab:swap}). Started
from RasterFairy's layout, it keeps only improvements, but each iteration recomputes the full
cost in Python. A million iterations take 63\,s and remove a median of 26\% of
RasterFairy's excess (7--91\%; most on the near-line, least on Gaussian and uniform points). Ten million iterations
(10 minutes) remove a median of 43\% (13--96\%). Even then the excess is 4--320 times that of
the fast preset, which takes 0.012\,s, and below that of BSP-OT with 64 plans on only four
distributions. The dense SciPy solver finds the
optimum in 2.7\,s. Started from a random permutation, as in upstream's demo, a million
iterations leave 0.4--6300\% excess.

\paragraph{With all cores.} On 24 hardware threads, \ShuffleSnap\ ran 7--14$\times$ faster than on
one and returned bit-identical assignments in all 270 paired runs. At $10^5$ points the
median time is 0.09\,s for fast, 0.19\,s for the default and 0.53\,s for quality.
BSP-OT gained 4--7$\times$ (0.13\,s with 16 plans, 0.41\,s with 64); its plans are computed
in parallel, but the cycle-merging pass that combines them is largely sequential. At 24
threads the fast preset is therefore faster than BSP-OT with 16 plans (0.09\,s against
0.13\,s), and the default is faster than BSP-OT with 64 plans (0.19\,s against 0.41\,s).

\paragraph{Exact solutions.} Started from \ShuffleSnap's default preset, the certified solver
finished every instance, in 17\,s (uniform points) to 23\,min (16 duplicated sites), with a
median of 117\,s. That is 10--620 times the time of the default preset (median 32 times).
Started from bisection instead, it failed on four instances (mixture, duplicates,
near-line, spiral): no certificate within an hour or 50 pricing rounds. On the six
instances where it did certify, it was 1.2--27 times slower. The start matters because the
candidate edges come from it: \ShuffleSnap's layout is close enough to optimal that few
pricing rounds are needed. A dense exact solver is not an option at this size. Already at
$N=10^4$, SciPy needed 11--352\,s (Table~\ref{tab:main10k} in the appendix), whereas the
certified sparse solver needed 0.5--6.9\,s.
