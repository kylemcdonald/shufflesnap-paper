\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Where it works.} Coarse-to-fine strided windows get very close
to the optimum for the smooth, clustered layouts that motivate the problem. On the two
real 70{,}000-point UMAP embeddings, the default preset is within \RealBalancedExcess\ of
the certified optimum and visually indistinguishable from it. The method needs no dense
matrix, runs in 0.15--0.4\,s with all 24 threads at $10^5$ points (default preset), gives the
same result for any number of threads, and never increases the cost. At the running time of
BSP-OT with 16 or 64 plans, it is closer to the optimum on eight or nine of ten distributions.

\paragraph{Where it fails.} \ShuffleSnap\ is a local search, and its failures
follow from that. (i)~Near-uniform data stay hardest: the default is 0.84\% above the
optimum at $10^5$ points, and its excess grows with $N$ (0.1\% at $10^3$ points, 1.3\% at
$10^6$). The excess consists of a few long closed loops of one-cell shifts that no
window can see whole. Constructed starts make the relative excess unbounded, but a random
start gives a draw from a narrow distribution that hardly depends on the input: at $10^4$
uniform points, 90\% of 2{,}500 runs were 0.55--1.38\% above the optimum. (ii)~Masks with concave bays and grids with surplus cells need global
rerouting of mass, which coarse schedules get wrong: on the S-shaped mask, points drawn
inside the S end up lined along its bays. The default's larger windows and $\sqrt2$ schedule
reduce this (1.4\% on the S with uniform points, against 9.3\% for BSP-OT), but surplus cells
remain hard (3.1\% with less than a row of surplus), and a mask can even cut exchanges
between usable cells off entirely. (iii)~Structured starts do not help: bisection starts
hurt on every input, and row-sorted starts on most.

\paragraph{The exact solver changes the question.} We built the certified solver as an
evaluation tool. It turned out to be practical. Starting from \ShuffleSnap's answer, it proved optimality
at $10^5$ points in 17\,s to 23\,min on one core (median about 2\,min), and at $10^6$
uniform points in 11\,min.
When a certified optimum is worth the wait, it is the better choice at this scale, and
\ShuffleSnap's role is then that of a fast, good-enough answer, a warm start, and a
method for sizes and time budgets where exact solving is out of reach. We report this
even though it narrows the case for the method we set out to test.

\paragraph{Displacement is not neighbourhood.} On the real embeddings, BSP-OT comes within
2--9\% of the optimal displacement, yet its layouts are locally scrambled
(Figure~\ref{fig:visual}, row 2), and it halves both neighbourhood measures. RasterFairy
keeps local order inside its blocks and breaks it along block seams. Even the optimum
tears neighbourhoods along cluster boundaries (Figure~\ref{fig:visual}, row 5). If
neighbourhood preservation is the goal, it should be optimized or at least measured
directly, e.g.\ with LAS/FLAS and DPQ~\citep{barthel2023improved}. A lower displacement
does not guarantee it.

\paragraph{Which method to use.} In our measurements:
\begin{itemize}
\item If a proven optimum is worth minutes of computation, use the certified solver
  started from \ShuffleSnap. We tested it up to $10^6$ uniform and $2\times10^5$ clustered
  points.
\item For interactive use, the default preset gives near-optimal layouts of $10^5$ points
  in about 0.2\,s on a multicore machine, or 2.3\,s on one core; the fast preset takes
  0.09\,s or 0.77\,s, about as long as BSP-OT with 16 plans, and is closer to the optimum on
  eight of ten distributions.
\item Under very tight budgets (below 0.12--0.36\,s on one core at $10^5$ points), BSP-OT
  gives the best result available. With 24 threads we found no such budget.
\item When the position of empty cells does not matter, an exact-fit mask is both simpler
  and handled far better than surplus cells, by every method.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Limitations of this study.} Everything ran on one workstation, CPU only.
BSP-OT has a CUDA implementation that we did not test. The objective is squared
Euclidean displacement in 2D. The window solver is exact for any cost, but its geometric
start is derived for squared distances, and we did not evaluate other costs or 3D grids. The certified solver depends on OR-Tools and on
integer scaling. Its certificate accounts for the scaling, but its running time depends
on the candidate set and the warm start. Real data consist of two UMAP embeddings with
one parameter setting. The billion-point run is a single instance
and has no known optimum. The bbox normalization is part of the problem definition; other
normalizations change the optimum. Timings exclude warm-up (imports, thread start-up) and follow one
pinning policy, and unrelated background services were running (load about 1). We
compared against the representative settings of each comparator, but not every setting.
In particular, RasterFairy's swap optimizer was tested only at $N=2{,}500$: it needs dense
$N\times N$ memory, and its PyPI release does not run on Python~3.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The Shellsort idea largely works. Strided exact windows,
refined from coarse to fine, bring clustered layouts, including two real UMAP embeddings
of 70{,}000 points, to within hundredths of a percent of a proven optimum in a
fraction of a second on a multicore machine, with little memory and deterministic parallelism. The idea also has sharp
limits. Its remaining error forms long loops that no window can see, which makes uniform
data, lattices, concave masks and surplus cells hard, with an unbounded worst case. Window
size matters most, and a window solver that starts from closed-form transport duals lets
\ShuffleSnap\ use windows large enough to beat BSP-OT at equal running time. The most consequential finding may be the
exact solver built to judge
the heuristic: seeded with \ShuffleSnap's answer, proven optima are within reach at
$10^5$--$10^6$ points. For point-to-grid layout, exactness is therefore a matter of
minutes, not a matter of impossibility.
